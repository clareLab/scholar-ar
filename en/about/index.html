Abstract

AllRankings ranks research institutions in every area of science by their papers in the strongest venues of that area. This text follows one real paper through each step that turns it into the numbers on the site. The paper is split among areas by the primary topics of the works it cites. Each area orders its venues by their Open Scholar Ranking score, a measure built on citation flows between venues, and T1 holds the strongest venues, which together publish about a twenty-seventh of the area’s papers. Count adds up an institution’s fractional credit for the papers in the selected tiers and years, and Authors estimates how many of its people publish in the area in an average year. The table ranks institutions by Excess, their Count minus the Count expected at their size from a power law in Authors fitted to the whole area. In all 27 areas tested, papers in a higher tier have a higher mean field-weighted citation impact and are more often among the most cited 1%. They are more often among the most cited 10% in 26 of the 27 areas. This is the main evidence that the tiers carry meaning.

Introduction

AllRankings, at https://ar.clarelab.moe, ranks research institutions by their papers in selected venues, in every area of science. It follows CSRankings [3], which ranks computer science departments by their papers at a hand-picked list of selective conferences and splits each paper’s credit evenly across its co-authors. To cover every area, AllRankings picks the venues by a fixed rule on citation-based venue scores and paper volume, places each paper in areas through the works it cites, and passes credit to institutions by fractional counting over authors and their listed institutions, as the Leiden Ranking does for whole universities [4, 5].

An area is a node of the OpenAlex topic hierarchy [1, 2], which has 4 domains, 26 fields, 252 subfields and 4,516 topics. The site calls the whole of science All and each node a view. The children of All are the domains, the children of a domain are its fields, and so on down to the topics, which are the leaves.

The sections below follow one paper through the rules that turn its references into Count at an institution. They then define Authors, Per author and Excess, describe the tables and test the tiers against citation impact. Side findings and a short account of how the site runs close the text, and every number and figure comes from the data release named under the title.

The paper set

The source is the OpenAlex snapshot of 2026-10-14 [1]. AllRankings takes the papers that meet five conditions.

  • Published from 2004 to 2025.
  • Of type article, review or conference-paper.
  • In a venue with an Open Scholar Ranking score in at least one of these years.
  • Free of the retraction and paratext flags of OpenAlex.
  • Citing at least one work with a primary topic, read from the 1990 to 2025 works of the main OpenAlex corpus [2].

A venue is an OpenAlex source, and a paper belongs to the venue of its primary location and to its publication year. OSR scores journal and conference sources, so proceedings that OpenAlex files as book series, such as Lecture Notes in Computer Science, fall outside the set. OpenAlex sometimes splits one conference across several source records, each then scored and tiered on its own. Its records of NeurIPS, ICML and ICLR hold a small share of these conferences’ papers, and the ACL record covers part of its years. Every paper of the set counts towards Authors, and a paper enters the tiers and Count in the years its venue has a score.

The set holds 58,479,177 papers in 118,987 venues. Their authorships name 25,955,741 people with a listed institution, at 114,402 institutions, and 8,587,323 of these people have an ORCID iD. Together the papers make 1,716,592,184 references to works with a primary topic.

An authorship pairs an author with the institutions listed for that author on the paper at publication, so each paper stays with the institutions its authors named at the time. A person is an ORCID iD [6] when the OpenAlex author record has one and the OpenAlex author id otherwise, so author records that share an ORCID iD form one person.

The pipeline picks it by a fixed rule: a paper of 2024 in a venue that is T1 in Computer Science that year, whose moderate reference list places at least half of the paper in that field and reaches at least three fields. Venue scores

Venue scores come from Open Scholar Ranking (OSR) [8], at https://osr.clarelab.moe. OSR fits a Bradley-Terry model [9] to citation flows between venues, in the line of Stigler [10] and Varin and colleagues [11], with a random walk over years as in dynamic Bradley-Terry models [12]. A citation flows from the citing work’s source to the cited work’s source in the citing work’s publication year. A venue with higher φ\varphiφ draws more of the citations it exchanges with other venues, after allowing for how much each side cites and how much it offers to be cited, and the scores for year ttt come from a fit on the years from 2004 to ttt. Tiers

Every view at every level of the hierarchy sets its own tiers for each year from its own papers. The mass of venue vvv in view nnn and year yyy adds up each paper’s share in every child ccc of nnn, over the venue’s papers of that year,

Mv,y(n)=∑p∈v,  y(p)=y  ∑csc(p), M_{v,y}(n) = \sum_{p \in v,\; y(p) = y} \; \sum_{c} s_c(p), Mv,y​(n)=p∈v,y(p)=y∑​c∑​sc​(p),

with each child’s share counted in millionths of a paper before the sum, and a topic view takes the share in its topic. The pool holds the venues with a score in year yyy and a positive mass in the view, ordered by φ\varphiφ from the highest, with ties going to the lower OpenAlex source id. Let M1,…,MmM_1, \dots, M_mM1​,…,Mm​ be the masses in this order and T=∑rMrT = \sum_r M_rT=∑r​Mr​ the view’s scored output in that year. The midpoint of venue rrr is the mass before it plus half its own,

cr=∑q<rMq+Mr2, c_r = \sum_{q < r} M_q + \frac{M_r}{2}, cr​=q<r∑​Mq​+2Mr​​,

and its tier is

τr={1if cr≤T/27,2if T/27<cr≤T/9,3if T/9<cr≤T/3,4otherwise. \tau_r = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } c_r \le T/27, \\ 2 & \text{if } T/27 < c_r \le T/9, \\ 3 & \text{if } T/9 < c_r \le T/3, \\ 4 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} τr​=⎩⎨⎧​1234​if cr​≤T/27,if T/27<cr​≤T/9,if T/9<cr​≤T/3,otherwise.​

The rule walks down the pool from the strongest venue, cuts the view’s output at one twenty-seventh, one ninth and one third, and places each venue by where the middle of its own papers falls. T1 thus holds about a twenty-seventh of the view’s scored output, the first two tiers about a ninth and the first three about a third. In a pool of 30 venues of equal mass the first venue is T1, the next two are T2, the next seven T3 and the rest T4, and when the venues at the top carry more mass the upper tiers hold fewer venues.

A venue holds a tier in every view where it has papers that year, so the top tiers of a field include general journals and strong journals of other areas whose papers reach the field. The same venue can be T1 in one field and T3 in another, and it can change tier from year to year.

Figure 2 applies the rule to Computer Science in 2024, where the pool holds 45,804 venues with a mass of 317,654 papers. Along the φ\varphiφ order T1 ends at rank 684, T2 at rank 2,347 and T3 at rank 8,042. The order opens with strong journals of other areas, led by The Quarterly Journal of Economics with a mass of 1.3 papers in the field. The 100 largest of the 684 T1 venues hold 93.1% of the T1 mass, and the largest alone holds 18.1%. Together the T1 venues hold 3.6% of the field’s scored output, close to a twenty-seventh. Nature Physics is among them, so the example paper counts in Computer Science under the default selection with its share of 23/27.

Selection

A selection in a view has four parts.

  • Years from y0y_0y0​ to y1y_1y1​ within 2004 to 2025, by default 2022 to 2025.
  • A set LLL of listings, each a pair of a tier and a venue. The Venues list shows each venue under every tier it holds in the view in the selected years, with one checkbox per listing, and checking a tier checks all its listings.
  • The checked children of the view. A topic view counts its own topic.
  • The selected countries, by default all of them.

A paper ppp of venue vvv published in year yyy counts if and only if y0≤y≤y1y_0 \le y \le y_1y0​≤y≤y1​ and (τ(v,y),v)∈L(\tau(v, y), v) \in L(τ(v,y),v)∈L, where τ(v,y)\tau(v, y)τ(v,y) is the tier of vvv in this view in year yyy. The default selects every T1 listing, every child and every country. A venue that is T1 in a field in the first two default years and T2 in the last two has two listings there, one under T1 and one under T2. By default its papers from the first two years count, and checking its T2 listing adds the later ones.

Authors

Authors estimates how many people at an institution publish in the area in an average year. It counts every author who lists an institution, in any position, whether known by an ORCID iD or by an OpenAlex author id.

Each person-year is one unit, and a person-year exists when a person has at least one authorship with an institution on a paper of the set in that year. Let P(a,y)P(a, y)P(a,y) be those papers of person aaa in year yyy. The unit is split evenly over P(a,y)P(a, y)P(a,y), each paper’s part evenly over the person’s institutions on that paper, and then over topics by the paper’s shares:

Per author and Excess

Per author is Count divided by Authors. Authors covers every paper of the set, so Per author grows with the share of a person’s work that reaches the selected venues. Count adds up over the selected years and Authors is a yearly mean, so Per author gives the selection’s papers per person publishing in an average year, and doubling the range at the same yearly output doubles it.

Count grows with the size of an institution, and Per author favours small institutions, where a few papers over a small Authors value make a large ratio. Excess compares each institution with the Count expected at its size in the view. It is the maximum-likelihood fit of a Poisson model in which ln⁡Expected\ln \mathrm{Expected}lnExpected is linear in ln⁡A\ln AlnA. Excess is in papers, read as the papers above or below what an institution of that size reaches in this view.

Excess sums to zero over the institutions of the fit. The fit takes in every institution of the view whatever the countries selected, so an institution has the same Excess in the world list and in its own country’s list. Each selection fits its own β\betaβ, so Excess over several years or several children differs from the sum of their separate values.

At the default selection β\betaβ is 1.27 in All, over 93,855 institutions, so an institution with twice the Authors of another is expected to reach more than twice its T1 Count. Figure 5 draws the fit. Among the fields β\betaβ is lowest in Veterinary, at 0.77, and highest in Economics, Econometrics and Finance, at 1.43. In All, Harvard University has the largest Excess, 3,990 papers above its Expected of 1,814.

The tables

The Institutions table lists every institution with a positive Count under the selection, after the country filter, with Count, Authors, Per author and Excess to two decimals and Excess signed. It sorts by Excess by default. Clicking another column header sorts by that column, largest first, and equal values share a rank. The flag shows the country OpenAlex records for the institution, and institutions with a blank country appear when every country is selected. A box before Count and one before Authors each keep the institutions above that column’s mean over the listed rows. Both boxes start empty, and ranks are positions in the list that remains.

Expanding an institution lists every person with positive credit on its counted papers. Tiers and citation impact

The tiers come from citation flows between venues, and this test measures the citation impact of the single papers in each tier. For each view and tier it takes the papers from 2016 to 2020 whose venue held that tier in the view in the paper’s year, weights each by its share in the view and computes three rates.

  • Top 10% share: the weighted share of papers that OpenAlex places in the top 10% by normalised citations [2, 14].
  • Top 1% share: the same for the top 1%.
  • Mean FWCI: the weighted mean of OpenAlex’s field-weighted citation impact, citations received over citations expected for works of the same type, year and subfield [2, 15].

Coverage is the weighted share of papers with all three values, and the window ends in 2020 so that the papers have had time to collect citations.

In Computer Science the top 10% share is 62.6% in T1 and 14.1% in T4. Mean FWCI drops from 9.06 in T1 to 1.37 in T4, with a coverage of 95% in T1. The top 1% share and the mean FWCI fall at every step from T1 to T4 in all 27 views, All and each field. The top 10% share does so in 26 of them, and in Decision Sciences its values for T2 and T3 are level, at 41.6% and 41.7%. Figure 6 shows every one of them.

Side findings

Composition and Concentration cover the default years, and Countries follows T1 Count one year at a time.

Composition

The All view tiers every venue by its whole output, and each field’s papers fall into these global tiers through their venues. Figure 7 splits each field’s output over the default years by the All view’s tier of its venues. Upstream fields, whose work much of science cites and builds on, place the largest parts of their output in the global T1, led by Neuroscience; Biochemistry, Genetics and Molecular Biology; and Immunology and Microbiology. Arts and Humanities places the smallest part there, followed by Dentistry, Veterinary and Nursing. In 2024 the T1 of All spans 845 venues with 3.7% of the year’s scored output, and its largest venue, Nature Communications, holds 6.8% of that T1.

Concentration

In All the 10 institutions with the largest T1 Count hold 6.7% of it and the 100 largest hold 31.6%. Over the institutions with positive T1 Count the Gini coefficient, twice the area between the Lorenz curve [16] and the diagonal, is 0.93, and Figure 8 draws the same measures for every field.

Countries

Country shares split T1 Count in All by the country of each institution, year by year. The share of the United States fell from 52.9% in 2004 to 28.4% in 2025. China’s share rose over the same years from 0.6% to 26.1%. Figure 9 draws the largest countries of 2025, all other countries together and the institutions with a blank country.

How the site runs

The site is a static page that reads Parquet files [17] from a static store with HTTP Range requests, fetching the parts of a file it needs. Each site build reads one release, named by the first 16 hexadecimal digits of a SHA-256 digest over the pipeline’s code, its configuration and the identities of its inputs.

A release holds the hierarchy, the names and countries of institutions and venues, each venue’s score per year and three files per view. The first gives each venue’s tier and mass per year and child. The other two hold running totals of Count and Authors in millionths from 2004 to each year, per institution and child and for Count also per tier. A selection from y0y_0y0​ to y1y_1y1​ reads the totals for y1y_1y1​ and y0−1y_0 - 1y0​−1 and subtracts, two row groups per checked tier and two for Authors. The view’s aggregates load at once, because the ranking and the fit of Excess need every institution.

The bundles hold one row per paper. A listing whose checkbox differs from its tier’s changes Count by one venue’s papers in some years, so the site loads that venue’s bundle for those years, recomputes the integers n(p,i,c)n(p, i, c)n(p,i,c) and adds or subtracts them. The product kc(p) w(p,i)k_c(p)\, w(p, i)kc​(p)w(p,i) stays below 2532^{53}253, so the browser’s double precision and the pipeline reach the same quotient and the same rounding. Expanding an institution loads its bundle for the selected years.

Before each deploy a check runs the site’s own data code in Bun against the local release. For a fixed set of selections it compares the Count the site would show with a separate recomputation from the bundles, exactly to the millionth, and any mismatch stops the deploy.
